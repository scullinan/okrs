# Objective Framework

Use this reference when coaching Objectives in more depth.

## ACCID

Before considering a Team Objective well-defined, ask:

- Is it Aligned with the unit's or company's goals and priorities?
- Is it Clear and specific enough for everyone to understand?
- Is it Challenging but still achievable?
- Is it Inspiring and motivating for the team?
- Is it Deadline-oriented to maintain focus and urgency?

If the team cannot answer yes to all five, refine the Objective.

## Aligned

A team Objective must align with its unit's goal. In larger organizations, a squad's Objective should support its tribe or business-unit priorities. In a small company without formal units, the Objective should contribute directly to the company's key focus.

Alignment question:

> Does this directly support our unit's or company's priorities?

Attractive work can still be misaligned. If an Objective is valuable in isolation but does not support the direct alignment anchor, flag it as a distraction risk.

## Clear

A clear Objective removes ambiguity and provides specificity. It should make success understandable without debate.

Challenge vague words such as improve, enhance, optimize, increase, drive, and support unless the Objective also explains what success means.

Clarity question:

> Would the team and its manager understand what success looks like?

Metrics are preferred when they improve clarity.

## Challenging

A good Objective stretches the team without feeling out of reach. If it is too easy, it becomes business-as-usual. If it is unrealistic, it becomes discouraging.

Challenging question:

> Does this require meaningful improvement, learning, or adaptation?

## Inspiring

An inspiring Objective creates an emotional connection. It may connect to customer impact, employee experience, product quality, team craft, or pride in doing meaningful work.

Avoid decorative motivation. Do not add fancy language to hide a dull metric.

Inspiration question:

> Does this excite the team, or does it sound like just another corporate metric?

Positive framing can help, but prevention or reduction wording is acceptable when it is clear, aligned, and motivating enough.

## Deadline-Oriented

For Team OKRs, the Objective itself needs a clear timeframe. A deadline turns an intention into a commitment and helps prevent drift.

KRs may inherit the Objective deadline when clear, but Objectives should not rely on KRs alone to create urgency.

Deadline question:

> By when does the team need to achieve this?

## Roofshot Realism

Team OKRs should usually be roofshots:

- Ambitious, but achievable.
- Bigger than business-as-usual.
- Smaller than moonshots.
- Realistic enough that the team can believe in it.
- Challenging enough to drive meaningful progress.

Use both evidence and team judgement:

- Baseline and prior performance.
- Capacity and competing work.
- Constraints and dependencies.
- Current roadmap and known commitments.
- Whether the team genuinely believes the Objective is realistically achievable.

If the team does not believe it is achievable, refine scope, target, timeframe, dependency assumptions, or wording.

## Zoom

Team Objectives need the right level of focus.

Use one of these qualitative labels:

- Too zoomed out: too broad, company-scale, or not actionable for the team.
- Balanced focus: meaningful team-level impact with enough room for the team to choose how to achieve it.
- Too zoomed in: too tactical, task-like, or better suited as a KR, initiative, or backlog item.

Zoom questions:

- Are we too zoomed out for the team to act?
- Are we too zoomed in, describing a task rather than the desired impact?
- Are we linking the team-level work to meaningful business, customer, product, or operational impact?
